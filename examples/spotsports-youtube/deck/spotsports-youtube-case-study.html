Report sports performance and price sponsorships on engaged views.

Measured

Across 746 sports channels, long-form videos published August 31 to September 29 had 74% more first-month public views per video (+60% to +94%) than the same channels’ summer videos. Some of that may be real growth.

Season or count?

Basketball and hockey rose alongside football and soccer before the NBA and NHL openers: imperfect checks, since basketball includes the WNBA playoffs and hockey training camps. No sport showed a clear Shorts rise.

Not measured

Engaged views, the old count. Each channel sees them in YouTube Studio; this analysis could not.

On August 24 YouTube changed what a view is, at the start of the busiest stretch of the sports calendar

Before

A view needed a viewer to keep watching

YouTube never published how long. That count is now called “engaged views” and is available in the creator’s YouTube Studio and through authorized channel reporting.

After

A view counts when the video starts

Any play counts, including a few seconds of autoplay in a feed. Shorts already counted this way. Counts from before August 24 were not restated.

Kickoff

Bad timing for sports

The American football and European soccer seasons were starting. Fall is when sports channels report season starts against last year, so a counting change is easy to read as a great start. Last year these weeks showed no long-form rise, a weak check (appendix A15).

Cross-season public-view records mix two counts. Post-change videos are overrepresented in this sample’s top 1,000

+74%

New videos vs the same creators’ summer videos

A new video’s first month runs far above the same creator’s earlier videos. Any recap that compares this fall with last fall on public views mixes two counts.

77%

Of the top 1,000 videos

Among long-form videos published May 8 to July 26 or August 31 to September 29, each at one month old, those after the change are 64% of uploads but 77% of the 1,000 most viewed. A uniform 1.74x adjustment cuts their share to 61%: a sensitivity to counting, not a measured correction. The windows also differ by season, summer against early fall, so part of the gap may be season.

Selling sponsorships: agree a CPM per 1,000 engaged views; convert public-view requests with each creator’s measured engaged share

Fee = views × share ÷ 1,000 × CPM

Public views × engaged share ÷ 1,000 × engaged-view CPM; e.g. 1,000,000 × 70% ÷ 1,000 × $30 = $21,000. Engaged share is each creator’s day-30 engaged ÷ public views in YouTube Studio; this study could not see it. Keep an existing engaged-view rate; agree metric and rate before renewing a public-view contract

For a guarantee, agree an engaged-view target and reconcile against actual engaged views

$21,000

Hypothetical 70% engaged share: 1 million public views hold 700,000 engaged views. Paying $30,000 means a $42.86 engaged-view CPM; at an agreed $30, the fee is $21,000, $9,000 less

The 70% share is hypothetical; substitute the creator’s measured share

Do not use 1 ÷ 1.74 to price a creator. Your engaged share: take your 10 most recent sponsored long-form videos published since August 24 that have reached day 30, and divide total engaged by total public views over each one’s first 30 days; disclose a smaller sample, and report Shorts separately. Football and soccer: base guarantees on comparable competition stages. NBA and NHL: mark opener guarantees provisional and rebase in late November, when October videos reach day 30 (A2). Packages: long-form and Shorts separately; the 74% is long-form only.

What would change this read, and when to look

Late November 2026

October videos, published as the NBA and NHL seasons start, reach one month old. Recheck the rise, and ask creators for public and engaged counts on the same videos. So far the Sept 7 week spiked to +106%, led by soccer (appendix A3); the Sept 21 week, at +64%, is back near the Aug 31 week (+67%), and videos from Sept 14 on run 11% below the first two post-change weeks (appendix A15).

What would settle it

What would settle it: public and engaged views on the same videos from a representative set of sports creators, matched by age. What we have: 19 re-cuts of public views all show a rise (+46% to +85%, appendix A17); none of them sees engaged views. Unexplained: the full step starts with videos published August 27, not August 24 (appendix A14).

The assumption

The evidence points to counting, but we cannot yet split the rise between counting and real viewing growth. The 74% rise cannot tell a creator’s engaged share or sponsorship discount: request public and engaged counts on the same sponsored videos, and agree the engaged-view CPM separately. Last year’s same weeks, the only long-form check that spans a season start, showed no rise (0.88x, 0.72x to 1.10x), though video ages differ widely (appendix A15).
