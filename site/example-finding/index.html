New users who got the onboarding checklist came back more often

For: Non-technical product owner · From: Dana Okafor · Generated 2026-07-20T14:05:00Z · Revision 1

Answer

Yes. New users who saw the onboarding checklist came back within a week more often than new users who did not, and the difference is big enough to keep the checklist.

The one thing that would change this

This is a fair comparison only because the two groups were assigned at random. If the checklist had gone to a particular kind of user (for example only iPhone users), the difference could be about those users, not the checklist. And the gap is one experiment's measurement: the 3 pp bar is a rule the team agreed in advance for acting on it, not a promise that the gap stays that size.

Decision it informs

Whether to keep the onboarding checklist for all new users or remove it. Dana Okafor owns the decision. Before the experiment, the team agreed the checklist earns its place if it lifts the share of new users who come back within a week by at least 3 pp.

Evidence

Cause and effect

New users who saw the checklist came back within a week more often than new users who did not: 34.8% compared with 28.5%.

Who is counted
Every person who signed up between the first of June and the twelfth of July (New York time) and was randomly given one of the two onboardings, 1,277 people in all.
Compared with
The people who were randomly given the old onboarding at the same time.
When
Signups from 2026-06-01 to 2026-07-12, and their app opens through 2026-07-19.
"Came back" means
Opened the app on at least one of the seven days after the day they signed up.

The gap is 6.3 pp, above the 3 pp the team agreed on before the experiment. Out of 624 people who saw the checklist, 217 came back. Out of 653 who saw the old onboarding, 186 came back.

Who was left out, and the limits of this claim
  • People who signed up before the first of June or after the twelfth of July were not part of the experiment and are not counted.
  • "Came back" says nothing about what they did in the app.
  • Six weeks of early-summer signups; a different season could behave differently.
How this number was calculated

Share who came back = people who opened the app on any of days one to seven after signup ÷ everyone who signed up in that group. Definition retained_7d version 2, approved in the team's own records. Query retention_by_arm; saved result retention_by_arm. The gap is the checklist share minus the old-onboarding share, in percentage points.

Pattern, not proof

The difference shows up on phones and not on the web: 8.7 pp on mobile against -1.3 pp on the web.

This split was not planned before the experiment. It is a follow-up look, so treat it as a lead to test, not a settled fact. On phones, 37.0% of checklist users came back against 28.3% of the others. On the web the two groups are small, so the web number moves a lot with a handful of people: 27.7% against 29.0%.

These are separate facts. Passing checks mean the arithmetic and the references hold; they do not by themselves make the conclusion right.

What would change our mind

If the checklist group were not at least 3 pp ahead of the other group, with at least five hundred people in each group, the checklist did not help enough to keep. That test is written down and passed on this data.

On a re-check, the main claim is re-tested on the same six weeks of signups with the same definition; it holds if the gap is still at least 3 pp. It cannot be re-tested if the definition changes or if there is no longer a group without the checklist. The phone-versus-web claim is a judgement for the next experiment, not a number to re-test; Dana Okafor decides.
